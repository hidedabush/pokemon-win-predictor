PREDICTING WHO WINS A POKEMON BATTLE
COSC 4368 Final Project - Project overview
Alexander Mayer, Andy Nguyen, Nguyen Hang, Ruel Cabading
==========================================================================

In one sentence: before a competitive Pokémon battle starts, both players see
each other's six Pokémon. We train a model that looks at only those two teams
and estimates the chance that each side wins.


1. MOTIVATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The problem
  In Pokémon Showdown's most popular format (Gen 9 OverUsed, or "OU"), each
  player brings a team of six Pokémon. At "team preview", just before the
  first turn, both teams are revealed. Players often feel some matchups are
  won or lost right there. We test that with data: how much does the team
  alone tell you about who will win?

Why it matters
  - It separates team building from playing skill and luck. A battle is
    decided by the teams, the players' decisions and random events (critical
    hits, misses). How well the teams alone predict the winner shows how much
    team building matters.
  - It is a real, large and noisy problem. Over a million recent battles,
    partly random outcomes, and inputs that are sets of Pokémon rather than
    numbers make it a good test of the methods from class.
  - Probabilities are more useful than yes/no. "Team A wins 62% of the time"
    says more than "Team A wins", so we judge the probabilities, not just
    accuracy.

Who would benefit
  - Players: see which teams and matchups are strong or weak before they
    enter a game.
  - Commentators and tournament viewers: pre-match win odds, like in sports
    broadcasts.
  - Students and researchers: a complete machine learning project on real
    data, from raw logs to evaluated models.


2. PROBLEM DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

The task
  This is binary classification: given one battle, predict the probability
  that player 1 wins.

  Input           Player 1's six Pokémon and player 2's six Pokémon, as seen
                  at team preview
                  e.g. Great Tusk, Kingambit, Gholdengo, ... vs. Garganacl,
                       Raging Bolt, Iron Moth, ...
  Optional input  Both players' ladder ratings (a skill score)
                  e.g. 1512 vs. 1498
  Output          P(player 1 wins), a probability from 0 to 1
                  e.g. 0.58
  Label           Who actually won the battle
                  e.g. 1 (player 1 won)

  We train two versions: "teams only", which answers the main question, and
  "teams + ratings", which shows how much knowing the players' skill adds.

Dataset
  - Battles: the public Hugging Face dataset
    HolidayOugi/pokemon-showdown-replays, which holds about 34 million Pokémon
    Showdown replays (73 GB). It contains 2.5 million Gen 9 OU battles. We
    keep the 1,190,747 battles played from June 2025 to October 2026.
  - What we keep from each replay: only the log lines with the two players
    and their ratings, the twelve Pokémon at team preview, and the winner
    (6 GB shrinks to 169 MB).
  - Data quality: 99.0% of battles list exactly six Pokémon per side, 100%
    record a winner, and 96.9% have a rating for both players. Battles
    failing any check are dropped.
  - Filtering: to focus on serious games, we keep battles where both players
    are rated at least 1300 and their ratings are within 100 points of each
    other, then use about 100,000 of them.
  - Pokémon facts: each Pokémon's types and base stats, and the type chart
    (which types are strong against which), come from Pokémon Showdown's own
    data via the poke-env library.
  - Train / validation / test split by time: the oldest 70% for training, the
    next 15% for tuning (validation), the newest 15% for the final test. Like
    in real use, we predict future battles from past ones, so the model can't
    "peek" at the future.


3. METHODOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Approach
  We use supervised learning on hand-built features. A team is a set of six
  Pokémon, so we first turn each battle into a row of numbers that describe
  the two teams, and then train standard classifiers on those rows. We chose
  this over a large neural network because the features are easy to explain,
  the models train in minutes, and the results map directly to concepts from
  class.

Features: describing a matchup with numbers
  Most features are written as "team A minus team B", so a positive number
  means player 1 has more of it.

  Stats      Total and average base stats of each team (HP, Attack, Defense,
             Sp. Atk, Sp. Def, Speed)
  Types      How many Pokémon of each of the 18 types each team has
  Matchups   How many of the opponent's Pokémon each team can hit
             super-effectively
  Species    Which exact Pokémon are on each team (+1 for team A, -1 for
             team B)
  Ratings    Player 1's rating minus player 2's rating (only in the
             "teams + ratings" version)

  Swap trick: in the training data we add every battle a second time with the
  two teams swapped and the label flipped. This way the model can't learn
  "player 1 usually wins" and has to learn from the teams.

Models, and why we chose them
  - Simple baselines: coin flip, "higher total stats wins", "more type
    advantages wins" and "higher rating wins". A real model has to beat these
    to be worth anything.
  - Logistic regression: our main model. It outputs a probability directly,
    each feature gets a weight we can read and explain, and L2 regularization
    stops it from overfitting to rare Pokémon.
  - Random forest and gradient boosting: tree models can pick up
    combinations, such as two Pokémon that are strong together, which
    logistic regression can't. They also let us discuss the bias-variance
    trade-off.
  - Small neural network (optional): only if time allows, to check whether a
    more flexible model finds anything extra.

Pipeline
  1. Download: fetch only the Gen 9 OU battles and keep only the lines we
     need.
  2. Parse: turn each log into one clean row: two teams, two ratings and the
     winner.
  3. Split: sort by date and cut into train, validation and test.
  4. Build features: turn each pair of teams into the numbers above.
  5. Train and tune: fit each model on train and pick its settings (such as
     regularization strength or tree depth) on validation.
  6. Evaluate once on test: compare all models and both input settings.

How we measure success
  - Accuracy: how often the predicted favorite actually wins.
  - Log loss and Brier score: how good the probabilities are. A confident
    wrong prediction is penalized much more than a cautious one.
  - Calibration plot: when the model says 60%, does that side win about 60%
    of the time?
  - Ablation: remove one feature group at a time to see which kind of
    information helps most.

  We expect the teams alone to give a real but modest edge over a coin flip,
  because player skill and luck also matter a lot. Measuring the size of that
  edge is itself one of our results.
